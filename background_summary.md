## Key Terms You Need to Know

| Term | What It Means |
|------|--------------|
| **Linguistic colonization** | When colonial powers forced their language on Indigenous peoples |
| **Heritage language** | A minority language spoken at home, tied to cultural identity |
| **Intergenerational transmission** | Passing a language down to children — the key to survival |
| **Three-Generation Rule** | Heritage languages often die by the 3rd generation of immigrants as kids shift to the dominant language |
| **Language revitalization** | Community-driven efforts to restore a dying language (different from *revival* of a dead one) |
| **Subtractive bilingualism** | When learning a dominant language erodes your native one |
| **Coloniality of language** | How colonial-era power structures continue shaping linguistic inequality today |
| **Linguistic subordination** | The prejudice that treats minority language varieties as "inferior" or "incorrect" |
| **Multilingual Education (MLE)** | UNESCO's framework for teaching children in their mother tongue to improve outcomes and preserve languages |

## Madagascar's Linguistic Situation

Understanding Madagascar's actual language context is essential for representing the country well.

### The Languages of Madagascar

**Malagasy** is the national language of Madagascar, spoken by virtually the entire population (~25 million people). This is actually quite unusual in Africa — Madagascar is one of the few countries where a native language truly dominates at the national level, rather than a colonial European language.

However, the picture is more complex:

- **Official languages**: Both **Malagasy** and **French** are official. English was briefly added as a third official language in 2007 but removed again in 2010.
- **French** remains the language of higher education, formal business, and much of the government — a direct legacy of French colonization (1896–1960).
- **Malagasy dialects**: There are about 18 recognized dialects of Malagasy across the island. The standard written form (*Merina Malagasy*, based on the dialect of the central highlands) is dominant in schools and media, while regional dialects risk marginalization within their own country.
- **Linguistic origins**: Malagasy is an Austronesian language, most closely related to languages spoken in Borneo — a reminder of Madagascar's unique settlement history, distinct from mainland Africa.

### Colonial History and Language

France colonized Madagascar in 1896 and immediately imposed French as the language of administration and formal education. This is a textbook example of **linguistic colonization**: Malagasy was downgraded, French was elevated, and speaking the colonial language became a marker of status and access to opportunity.

After independence in **1960**, Madagascar worked to reclaim Malagasy — a process called **"malgachization."** In the 1970s, the socialist government of Didier Ratsiraka aggressively promoted Malagasy in schools and public life. However, this policy had mixed results: it reduced French fluency among a generation of students, which some argue hurt economic development, while others see it as essential cultural decolonization.

Today, French-medium education persists, and many young Malagasies face **subtractive bilingualism** — their French instruction can come at the expense of deep Malagasy literacy, especially in rural areas.

### The Threat from Globalization

Madagascar is also subject to the same global pressures the committee is examining:

- **English** is increasingly present due to tourism, international NGOs, and global media — adding a third layer of linguistic pressure.
- Economic necessity pushes young people toward French and English, since those are the languages of formal employment and international opportunity.
- Regional Malagasy dialects face the same internal pressure that minority languages globally face — the standard dialect crowds them out in schools and media.

## Madagascar's Likely Position in Committee

Madagascar has strong, authentic reasons to advocate for robust language preservation policies. Here is the position you would logically defend:

**1. Champion multilingual education.** Madagascar's experience with post-independence "malgachization" — both its successes and failures — gives it real credibility on the debate about mother-tongue-based education. Madagascar can argue for UNESCO's Multilingual Education framework as essential for both cultural preservation *and* educational quality.

**2. Acknowledge the colonial wound.** Madagascar experienced direct French linguistic colonization. It can speak to the ongoing "coloniality of language" — how even after formal independence, French remains embedded in the structures of power, education, and opportunity.

**3. Support the International Decade of Indigenous Languages (2022–2032).** Madagascar would support UNESCO's Global Action Plan, especially the pillars around education, cultural heritage, and digital empowerment of indigenous languages.

**4. Raise the internal diversity issue.** Madagascar can bring a nuanced point: even within a country where a native language dominates nationally, internal dialects and minority speech communities face erasure. Language preservation isn't only an anti-colonial issue — it's also about protecting regional diversity *within* nations.

**5. Connect to sustainable development.** Madagascar is one of the world's biodiversity hotspots. Indigenous ecological knowledge is encoded in regional language and dialect. Language loss = knowledge loss about local ecosystems. This aligns with SDG 13-15 (climate/ecosystems) from UNESCO's framework.

## The Three Historical Eras to Know

The background guide frames linguistic history in three eras. Here's how Madagascar fits each:

| Era | Global Pattern | Madagascar's Story |
|-----|---------------|-------------------|
| **Colonial (17th–mid-20th c.)** | European powers forced their languages on colonized peoples | France colonized Madagascar in 1896; French imposed on administration and education |
| **Post-Colonial (1945–1991)** | New nations rebuilt identity; some pushed back against colonial languages | Madagascar's "malgachization" of the 1970s — reclaiming Malagasy as official and educational language |
| **Globalization (1991–present)** | English and dominant global languages pressure heritage languages further | French + English economic pressures; regional Malagasy dialects face internal erosion |

## Case Studies from the Background Guide (Know These)

The committee will likely reference these — understand them so you can apply them in debate:

**Marie Wilcox & Wukchumni (California):** A woman who, as the last fluent speaker, created a comprehensive dictionary of her dying language. Lesson: *community-driven* documentation and revitalization works — and technology can help. By 2021, new fluent speakers existed because of her effort.

**Soviet Russification (1930s–1991):** The USSR systematically suppressed ~125-150 minority languages by making Russian mandatory in schools and public life. Lesson: *state-enforced linguistic homogeneity* is one of the most destructive forces for heritage languages — and its effects outlast the policy by generations.

**Indigenous Boarding Schools (US & Canada):** Children were forcibly removed from families and forbidden from speaking native languages, with physical punishment enforced. Lesson: *linguicide is a form of cultural genocide*, and the trauma of forced language loss persists intergenerationally.
