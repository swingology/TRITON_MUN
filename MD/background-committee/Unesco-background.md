1. Linguistics
“The study of human speech including the units, nature, structure, and modification of language”
(Merriam-Webster.com)
2. Coloniality of Language
Anibal Quijano’s concept of coloniality of power* (“foundational power structures of the modern
world system that persist today”) in terms of linguistic dynamics established during colonization
that impact language in post-colonial societies today.
* Quijano’s concept is derived from Latin American history but can be expanded to explain the
linguistic histories of any colonized group/region.
3. Linguistic Colonization
Linguistic colonization refers to the direct action of enforcing linguistic assimilation/adoption of
European language upon colonized peoples.
4. Linguistic Restrictionism
Policies, movements, and ideologies aimed at enforcing linguistic homogeneity in a given
society. This is often used in the context of minority/immigrant languages in preference of a
dominant language.
5. Heritage Language
A minority language spoken in the society of a different dominant language; often spoken at
home or within a community reflects the cultural identity of speakers.
6. Intergenerational Transmission
The act of passing a language on to children and the following generations.
9
7. Three- Generation Rule
The theory that a heritage language in immigrant families is lost by the third generation in favor
of the dominant language.
1. First generation speaker: Referring to the immigrants who have strong proficiency in
their native tongue.
2. Second generation speaker: Bilingual children of immigrants, who exclusively use
heritage language at home for communication with first generation, but use dominant
language in public settings
3. Third generation speaker: Grandchildren of immigrants, children of second generation
who are often monolingual speakers of the dominant language with little to no
proficiency in heritage language
8. Globalization
“The development of an increasingly integrated global economy marked especially by free trade,
free flow of capital, and the tapping of cheaper foreign labor markets” (Merriam-Webster.com).
9. Language Revitalization
Community- driven efforts to restore the vitality of an endangered language for the purpose of
strengthening cultural identity, social cohesion, and reversing effects of historical oppression.
Revitalization is different from language "revival" which refers to revisiting dead languages with
no native speakers, and often lacks cultural connection (e.g., Latin), unlike revitalization efforts
often growing from within a cultural community, and often having at least a few elderly speakers
remaining.
10. Official Language
10
Official Language is a language that has been designated a legal right to be used in government
and legalities.
11. National Language
National Language is a language that has state-wide recognition as a representation of identity
for citizens. A national language is meant to strengthen linguistic unity among citizens.
12. Subtractive Bilingualism
The loss of proficiency in a native language due to learning a dominant secondary language in its
favored sphere.
13. Language Status
The relative public opinion of a language and/or dialect. Language status can vary from
perspective, but it is most commonly used in the context of linguistic discrimination.
14. Principle of Linguistic Subordination
A form of prejudice that evaluates certain language varieties and dialects as inferior and/or
“incorrect” to the dominant variety/dialect by methods of speaking, grammar syntax, etc.;
oftentimes a result of historical discrimination towards speakers of a certain variety/dialect.

Historical Background
Ethnographers estimate the presence of over 7,000 languages in the world, 40% of those
estimated to be endangered by UNESCO. Today, the most pressing issue for language
conservation is globalization. Languages with economic power in a certain region, often meaning
governmental official languages, in which business affairs are conducted, result in dominance of
said language due to demand for communication. Therefore, in a globalized world,
socioeconomic, political, and cultural factors are the determinants for language survival and
endangerment (Campbell & Muntzel, 1989). Speakers of minority languages may see dominant
languages as more appealing for job prospects, conducting civil affairs, or legalities. However, in
the context of historical initiatives (systemic erasure, imperialism, etc.), a huge factor behind
interrupting inter-generational transmission of heritage languages emerges from fear of
discrimination. During times of erasure initiatives, including colonialism, state-sponsored
homogeneity initiatives, and systemic discrimination, harmful rhetoric towards speakers of
heritage languages develops, and therefore generates negative public opinions of speakers,
discouraging the use of and passing on the language (Fernando et al., 2010).
We will examine different eras of concentration that contextualize different types of
linguistic erasure; colonial, post-colonial, and globalization.

OST OLONIAL RA
The post-colonial period is marked by the beginning and end of the Cold War, as new
independent nations took on the challenge of building an identity away from colonial influence
while also navigating where to align themselves in the ideological battle between the United
States and the Soviet Union. This is an important period in linguistics history because it focuses
on both linguistic debates in post-colonial nations as well as Soviet imperialism and
Russification of Soviet territories (Dostal & Knippenberg, 1992). For the purpose of this
committee, we will separate the Post-Colonial Era from Globalization with the end of WWII to
the fall of the Soviet Union, as these events defined policy across the world.
The nuances of Soviet Russification are held in how large and diverse those residing in
Soviet territories are culturally and linguistically. In 2020, the modern borders of Russia were
estimated to contain 125-150 minority languages spoken within its borders, with 62 being
classified as critically endangered (Jankiewicz et al., 2020). The Soviet Union spread the Russian
language from Eastern Europe to Asia, and has created a unique linguistic history for each
post-Soviet country.

LOBALIZATION RESENT
The fall of the Soviet Union left many post- Soviet nations with a convoluted linguistic
identity- many wanted to revert to heritage languages and foster revitalization efforts; however,
this was challenged by the growth of globalization that favored dominant languages such as
Russian. Communication is being redefined by technology that creates instantaneous messages.
As business and commerce become increasingly intertwined and facilitated by modern
technology, economic concerns make dominant languages valuable. As large global powers such
as the United States, China, and historically the UK and Russia have disproportionately
influenced global trade, large- scale technological development, and therefore, linguistic
hierarchies in the economic and scientific spheres. Globalization tends to reflect existing power
structures; with modern globalization, we are seeing the linguistic and cultural influence of
dominant nations spread across the world alongside economic, technological, and political
influence (Phillipson, 1992).
Anglophone globalization is the result of the United Kingdom’s colonial expansion, and
has been carried on by the United States, Canada, and other post-colonial nations that have
received the English language as a result. The United States, however, has furthered this
globalization of English by dominating technological and military spheres. The influence of
English overseas since the Cold War is mostly a result of American militarization (Crystal,
2003). In the 21st century, English has further expanded with technology and increasingly
intertwined global activity, referring to the growth of social media. de Swann (2001) described

Past UN Involvement
UNESCO has long been dedicated to language preservation. Dating back to 1953, with
UNESCO’s The Use of Vernacular Languages in Education, the organization has been resolute
in advocating to improve learning outcomes and preserve linguistic and cultural diversity in the
educational system through Multilingual Education. This stance was further reinforced in the
UNESCO General Conference in 1999, where UNESCO urged Member States to promote
multilingual education, and in 2003 where Education in a Multilingual World acknowledged
multilingualism as a global reality, and that the inevitability of multilingualism should be
channeled into opportunities for education (UNESCO 2025).
UNESCO has also been mobilizing efforts in transforming education policies and
practices to support Multilingual Education (MLE) guidance for young learners. On International
Mother Language Day, UNESCO reports demonstrate the positive impact of integrating
multilingualism in primary education systems for children to learn in their mother tongue
(UNESCO 2025). MLE guidelines focus primarily on directing educational policymakers and
key educational stakeholders at national and subnational levels, in addition to the entire
education community, that includes teachers, school administrators, and educators of all levels.
The guidance according to UNESCO supports the integration of languages spoken by Indigenous
peoples, ethnic minority groups, migrants, and refugees, and is in reference to UNESCO’s
Education in a Multilingual World position paper in 2003. Steps policymakers and implementers
can take in accordance with UNESCO’s guidance initiate practical actions regarding laws and
policies, curriculum, teacher recruitment, educational facilities and learning spaces, monitoring
and evaluation, and developing partnerships with key stakeholders (UNESCO 2025).
15
To spread awareness of the challenges Indigenous Peoples and their important role in
preserving their native languages, culture, and knowledge, the UN established the Global Action
Plan of the International Decade of Indigenous Languages in 2019. The Decade, ranging from
2022 - 2032, will be led by UNESCO, in which the agency makes a global call to “preserve,
revitalize and promote Indigenous languages while mainstreaming linguistic diversity and
multilingualism into sustainable development efforts,” (UNESCO 2024). This decade of
appreciation aims to not only bring awareness to the endangered nature of indigenous languages,
but also is a decade to mobilize stakeholders to participate in a global action plan that looks to
equip indigenous languages across sociocultural, economic, environmental, legal, and political
domains. Ten interlinked themes are considered in the Global Action Plan: (1) Education that is
qualitative and inclusive in Indigenous languages, (2) application of Indigenous Peoples’
languages and knowledge to ending hunger, (3) Digital empowerment and the right of expression
in Indigenous languages, (4) health in Indigenous language framework, (5) public services in
indigenous languages, (6) Heritage and all forms of culture for Indigenous peoples, (7)
Indigenous languages’ contribution to the protection of biodiversity, (8) Economic and growth
and job opportunities for Indigenous peoples, (9) using the preservation, revitalization, and
promotion of Indigenous languages to promote gender equality and women’s empowerment, (10)
long-term public-private partnerships towards the preservation of indigenous languages
(UNESCO 2024).
In reference to UNESCO’s efforts to achieve Sustainable Development Goals (SDG),
MLE not only directly supports specific goals related to education, but also creates opportunities
to foster other SDGs. The goal to “ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote
lifelong learning opportunities for all” (SDG 4) is directly supported by MLE efforts to integrate
children’s mother tongue in education and instruction, opening up opportunities and breaking
down barriers to literacy and academic success (UNESCO 2025). Simultaneously, MLE presents
chances to foster sustainable economic development (SDG 8), reduce inequalities (SDG 10),
combat climate change (SDG 13), protect aquatic and land ecosystems (SDG 14-15), and
promote peaceful, inclusive societies (SDG 16) (UNESCO n.d).

ASE TUDY ARIE ILCOX AND UKCHUMNI
The Wukchumni dialect is a language variety of the Tule-Kaweah language, originating
in the central valley of California from the Yokutsan family, is a language that was on the verge
of death. In the late 1990s, Marie Wilcox (1933-2021) began documenting Wukchumni, as she
realized she was the last native speaker. For the next 20 years, Wilcox worked to put together a
dictionary of Wukchumni, in hopes the language could be revitalized. In 2014, journalist and
filmmaker Emmanuel Vaughan-Lee documented her efforts in a short film called “Marie’s
Dictionary,” bringing attention to her work. Her Wukchumni dictionary included written entries,
audio recordings to record pronunciations, expressions, phrases, cultural background, and word
descriptions. She started by writing from her memory, and over the years, her family had helped
her navigate a computer to organize her work. Typing with only her pointer fingers, Marie
produced one of the most comprehensive written records ever created for Wukchumni. This was
revolutionary, as many Indigenous languages (Wukchumni included) are traditionally only
transmitted orally without written recordings (Sun Gazette, 2021).
After the documentary film was released, it drew many eyes to Marie’s work in her small
town. Many journalism outlets began interviewing her and her family members to learn about
her process. While the language is still critically endangered, this symbolizes revitalization
efforts that are successful at starting a new life for dying languages.
She expanded her efforts in the community, teaming up with her daughter to lead
language classes as the local Owens Valley Career Development Center, and encouraged other
tribal members, especially the youth, to take initiative to utilize Marie’s efforts to learn
Wukchumni (Global Oneness Project, n.d.; Good.is, 2015). These teaching efforts helped bring
her dictionary from a document to a living resource for the community. By 2021, many members
of Wilcox’s family and others had become at least three fluent speakers of Wukchumni, and her
great-great-grandson was being raised with the language from birth (New York Times, 2021).
17
Marie passed away on September 25, 2021, in her hometown of Visalia, CA. Her efforts
have saved the vitality of the Wukchumni language; and even in the case that there were no new
learners/speakers, the documentation she accomplished has cemented the Wukchumni language
in history, and ensured it cannot be forgotten to history. This demonstrates a successful
revitalization attempt of a dying language, and therefore cultural preservation.
C S #2- S R
ASE TUDY OVIET USSIFICATION
In the Soviet Union (1922-1991), linguistic homogenization initiatives were central
features of nationality policy that attempted to erase the vast diversity that encompassed the
territory in the name of “building identity”; however, was weaponized as a tool in executing past
prejudices, and building new ones. Soviet Russification, as the process is named, began in the
1930s, as early Bolsheviks promoted policies of “korenizatsiya” (indigenization), that were
aimed at developing non-Russian languages for local elites, but under Stalin had advanced to
enforcing Russian as the lingua franca of the USSR (Martin, 2001). This new emphasis on the
Russian language was justified as an act of ‘unification’ or something ‘necessary’ for the
prosperity of the state. While this may be true, the diminishing of smaller, local languages in
favor of Russian was also used as a tool to reinforce the political and cultural dominance and
influence of the USSR over ethnically non-Russians in its borders (Smith, 1998).
Under Stalin, Soviet leadership associated centralized control of citizens with linguistic
unity, beginning mandates for Russian communication. In 1938, Russian language was required
for instruction in schools across the USSR. This marked a new era of linguistic policies in the
territory, as it drastically contrasted 1920s policies. It mirrored Tsarist Russification,
reawakening discriminatory stereotypes and notions surrounding non-Russian languages from
pre-Bolshevik times (Grenoble, 2003). Russian was promoted as the language of science,
administration, and education, and many heritage languages faced erosion over the next couple
decades.
18
After World War II (1945), Russification became more intense, mostly seen in the Baltic
states, Ukraine, and Central Asia. Soviet territories allowed for large- scale migration of Russian-
speaking populations across non- Russian republics, which altered demographic balances and
influenced perspectives on speakers of non-Russian languages, as well as enforcing Russian as a
lingua franca with further growth of the use of Russian in urban centers (Smith, 1998). Through
educational enforcement, technical fields became restricted to Russian speakers, reinforcing the
power of the language even in a communist state. Russia was associated with education,
modernity, economic advancement, and especially, eurocentrism (Blistein, 2001). Local
languages were put in jeopardy by Russification initiatives systemically minimizing their utility
in public life, revealing why ~148 languages within Russia are noted as (at least) endangered
(UNESCO).
The effects of Russification are still present today, as many people of post-Soviet nations
speak Russian as a second language. The cultural imperialism done by these initiatives, as
bilingualism was widespread, but often used as a discriminatory tool towards non-Russian
speaking groups. Speakers of a given heritage language were expected to learn Russian and
continue to face prejudices today in their own communities. This is why inter-generational
transmission of heritage languages is interrupted; this example provides both a systemic and
discriminatory reason for said interruption. With growing globalization, history is not repeating,
but rather expanding upon itself. Russian as a common language between millions of people
allows for business affairs and economic incentives. Globalization means that revitalization
efforts for heritage languages can be difficult, as it lacks incentives for (primarily) the youth to
learn.
C S #3- I B S
ASE TUDY NDIGENOUS OARDING CHOOLS
From the late 19th century onward, federal and church-run boarding schools
systematically removed Indigenous children from their communities with the goal of pure,
Eurocentric assimilation into American/Canadian society. Seen in both the United States and
Canada, a manifestation of heavy discrimination and violence towards Indigenous people since
the colonization of the Americas, these schools were an example of cultural erasure through
language. A central mechanism of these schools was the prohibition of native languages, and the
19
mandate of English. The use of heritage languages would result in corporal punishment, coercing
the speaking of the English language and the abandonment of native languages (Language Loss,
2019). The violence practiced in these schools with the enforcement of linguistic homogeny
constitutes a form of cultural genocide through linguicide, argue scholars
(IndigenousNetwork.org, 2022; American Academy of Arts and Sciences, 2025).
Within these schools, children were forbidden from speaking their languages, and the use
of English (or even French, in French-speaking Canadian territories) was enforced by not only
physical punishment, but also in cruel forms of isolation and humiliation (Facing History &
Ourselves, 2019; Courthouse News Service, 2025). These policies were a systemic example of
linguistic erasure, and stemmed from colonial ideologies that equated traits of Indigenous
cultures with “savagery” and European languages with “civilization”, demonstrating the
prejudiced ideas that Indigenous culture and identity had to be erased for “advancement”
(Broberg, 2025).
Today, scholars and Indigenous advocates highlight the link between language and
identity, demonstrating how language loss was not just a result of these schools, but a key
strategy of forced assimilation (IndigenousNetwork.org, 2022)

Closing Remarks
Overall, today’s status of global languages is a reflection of history. Throughout the eras
of colonization, post-colonial nation building, and what this committee aims to consider,
globalization, language has been ever-changing. The effects of colonization have left nations
with identity/linguistic crises, many of which have been expanded upon in the post-colonial era.
In globalization, language conservation looks different. We can’t break global interconnectivity,
however, we must notice that globalization can exacerbate pressures on heritage languages,
many of which have been under pressure since the colonial era. In this new era of technological
and economic advancements, it is important for us to consider how language can be preserved,
and therefore, how culture can be protected. Delegates should examine revitalization efforts,
such as Marie Wilcox, and study the successes and failures of methods taken. In studying
revitalization techniques, it is important delegates acknowledge the historical contexts of the
status of many endangered languages, because we must understand their background to preserve
them. Every nation on Earth has its own unique linguistic history, which is why delegates are
encouraged to study the background of their delegation’s history, as well as surrounding
countries. For this committee, we want delegates to ask themselves, how can language and
culture be preserved successfully, given the history of language, and current challenges posed by
globalization.
