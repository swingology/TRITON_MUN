Sensitivity & Harassment Policy:
TritonMUN is committed to fostering a healthy environment for diplomacy and dialogue, and
upholding standards of diversity, equity, and inclusion for all delegates. Harassment or disruptive
behavior of any kind—whether verbal or non-verbal, in or out of committee—is strictly
prohibited. All attendees and staffers should be treated with equal respect, and discrimination on
the basis of race, color, gender identity, sexual orientation, religion, nationality, ethnicity, or
physical/mental disability will not be tolerated. This list is not exhaustive, and should not be
treated as such. Please report any instances of harassment to conference staff, either directly
or through the Anonymous Feedback Form.
In addition, we acknowledge that topics discussed in-committee may be politically sensitive,
particularly in Specialized and Crisis Committees. While we understand that delegates may be
immersed in the action and setting of their committee, delegates must maintain decorum and
refrain from inflammatory language and actions, even when doing so may be historically
accurate to one’s character, group, or country. When drafting crisis directives, delegates should
refrain from pursuing any action that explicitly violates human rights, including—but not limited
to—enslavement and genocide. Please refer to your committee’s background guide for
specific sensitivity statements and guidelines, and direct any questions to the committee’s
head chair.
All attendants should observe these policies during the entirety of the conference, even outside
formal debate, including opening/closing ceremonies, “FUNMUN”, etc. Please note that
violation of these policies may result in consequences such as disqualification from awards and
contact towards the delegate’s Advisor. At TritonMUN, our goal has always been focused on the
delegate’s abilities to enjoy and have fun in committee. However, in order to truly enjoy
TritonMUN, delegates must feel safe in committee, which starts with respecting one another.

ULES OF ROCEDURE
NOTICE: This is not a typical specialized committee. Directives will NOT be passed or written,
contrary to what you might have experienced previously in other specialized committees. The
only documents to be considered in this committee are written amicus curiae briefs.
Roll Call:
Roll call is performed by the Chair at the beginning of every committee session. Because
SCOTUS does not use voting procedure to confirm decisions, members may only declare
themselves to be “present.” All justices are required to take side in an opinion.
Perpetual Moderated Caucus:
● As this is a specialized committee, perpetual moderated caucus will take place. The
standard speaking time will be one minute.
● The procedure is as follows:
○ Dais calls for delegates wishing to speak to raise placards.
○ Dais chooses any number of delegates to speak in order: each delegate will have
the standard speaking time.
○ These steps are repeated until the perpetual moderated caucus is closed or
suspended. The perpetual moderated caucus will be suspended if no delegates
choose to race their placards to speak: as such, we necessitate a minimum of one
delegate to speak each time the dais calls for those wishing to speak.
Precedence of Motions (Most to Least Disruptive):
1. Suspension of Debate
2. Present an Opinion
3. Closed-doors Judge Deliberation
4. Present an Amicus Curiae Brief
5. Present Evidence
8
6. Extension
7. Straw Poll
8. Unmoderated Caucus
9. Gentlemen’s Unmoderated Caucus
10. Round Table
11. Other Suspension of the Rules, entertained at dais discretion
Motions:
All motions may be made during any downtime between an exhausted speaker’s list of the
perpetual moderated caucus. A majority vote will be made accordingly. The dais reserves the
right to rule any motion dilatory at any time.
MOTIONS OF COMMITTEE:
Motion to Present an Opinion:
After the Majority Opinion and Dissenting Opinion are complete, the delegates may request to
present the opinion in front of the committee and dais. Following presentation of the opinion,
amendments will not be entertained, but a brief Q&A period may be deemed fit by dais
discretion.
Motion to Present Evidence:
Delegates wishing to present evidence must first input evidence into a shared document with the
dais. If the motion passes, the evidence presented will be shown in front of the committee, and
that delegate will have three minutes to describe the evidence and its implications.
a. Formal processes are required in order to present evidence in court. Prior to the motion,
the evidence that a side wishes to submit must undergo a brief review from the dais. We
want you to utilize evidence found through active research BEFORE the conference: you
may refer to legal codes of the time period, discuss precedent cases, etc. Once created,
the dais will review on a case-by-case basis to ensure the sufficiency, appropriateness,
and relevancy.
9
b. Upon dais approval, the evidence may be presented to the court. Representatives
submitting the piece of evidence will be given the aforementioned three minutes of
speaking time to summarize and add to their argument.
Motion to Present an Amicus Curiae Brief:
Delegates representing a side may call for another delegate in the role of an amicus curiae to be
called to the stand to provide an account of their opinion and summarize/elaborate on their
written brief. Delegates in the role of an amicus curiae considered to be an expert of their field
may provide delegate research-backed advice to the committee to be taken as facts into
consideration of the case.
This differentiates from speaking normally during the perpetual moderated caucus in the sense
that it should be used to OFFICIALLY present opinions and information advantageous to your
side: this may entail an expert opinion/account, or presentation of legal code or information that
the primary representative could not before.
a. Once an amicus curiae is called and provides their brief, the representative(s) of their side
will be permitted to ask three questions, to which the witness may respond to accordingly.
This is an opportunity to fortify your argument with further amicus curiae answers.
b. Afterwards, the opposing representatives will be allowed to ask three questions in a
cross-examination, to which the witness may respond to accordingly.
This is an opportunity to examine the validity and effectiveness of the amicus curiae’s
brief.
c. Each amicus curiae may present/hold their brief ONCE per committee session.
Motion for closed-doors Judge Deliberation:
Delegates in the role of justices may motion to hold a closed-door conference with all other
judges to discuss sensitive matters and opinions on the case. This may be done at any time during
aforementioned availability for motions.
a. This motion exists for judges to deliberate among themselves over presentation and
writing of majority and dissenting opinions, without further input from others.

Motion for an Unmoderated Caucus:
Delegates may request a brief period of informal debate in the form of an Unmoderated Caucus
to work on and converse with others. The dais may limit the number of times such requests are
entertained, by their discretion.

EY ERMS
1. United Steelworkers of America: A general trade union, including laborers for steel
mills and factories during the Korean War, who threatened strike for higher wages against
major U.S. steel producers.
2. Wage Stabilization Board: Independent agency of the U.S. government that sought to
make and influence wage control policies during the Korean War, created by the sitting
President Harry S. Truman. The Board held power to control wages of hourly employees,
albeit with extremely limited authority.
3. Tart-Hartley Act: U.S. Federal Law created and meant to restrict the power and
activities of labor unions. A distinct power of the act is the ability of the President to
issue an 80 day long injunction, halting a strike or lockout for a maximum of 80 days. It
was vetoed by President Truman only to be overridden by Congress.
4. Selective Service Act: U.S. Federal Law meant to function as a military mobilization
law. It introduced requirements for eligible men to register in draft. It also focused on the
procurement and allocation of military resources during mobilization, permitting
requisitioning property for military purposes with the according compensation to owners.
5. Injunction: A court-ordered directive requiring a party to perform or cease an action to
prevent immediate harm when financial compensation is inadequate to the plaintiff. Prior
to this landmark case, the plaintiffs were requesting an injunction for a restraining order.
6. Balance of Equities: Judicial principle used to determine if a remedy (such as an
injunction) is fair, weighing potential issues caused to the defendant as compared to the
benefit to the plaintiff. It acts to ensure fairness in disputes.
7. Stay Order: Court directive that pauses or completely halts an action, court proceeding,
or enforcement of a decision.
8. Certiorari: A writ or order made where a higher court reviews a lower court’s decision.
In this case, certiorari was petitioned for with the Supreme Court.
9. Amicus curiae: An individual or group permitted to assist the court with information,
expertise, and more related to the case at hand.

ISTORICAL ACKGROUND
I
NTRODUCTION
On June 27th, 1950, U.S. President Harry S. Truman ordered the deployment of U.S. air
and naval forces to assist South Korea shortly after North Korea began their invasion. This
marked the United States’ beginning of their official involvement in the Korean War. Rather than
using price controls similar to the ones set during World War II for the purpose of distribution of
scarce resources and preventing inflation, Truman chose to create the Wage Stabilization Board,
seeking to use this to control wages and subsequent consumer prices. However, upon the
proposal of higher wages for steel workers, companies rejected it unless permitted to raise prices
in a manner the government could not approve of. As such, unionized workers under the United
Steelworkers of America planned for strike in April of 1952. However, before the strike began,
Truman seized and nationalized steel plants with the same industry management in place to act
under the federal government’s guidance.
T A ’ C
HE DMINISTRATION S HOICE
Incapable of making a union and industry management compromise, Truman’s seizure of
the steel plants was announced with a radio and TV broadcast. There were other options opposed
to the direct seizure. Two other acts were potential choices, with the Tart-Hartley Act containing
national emergency provisions to prevent striking altogether and the Selective Service Act
permitting seizure, but on the grounds of the compliance process being too uncertain and lengthy.
The Tart-Hartley was rejected out of the administration’s previous disapproval of it, and the
Selective Service was rejected out of concerns for time constraint. Thus, with all options
exhausted, Truman turned to implied emergency-time power.

Shortly after the President’s speech, attorneys were sent by the steel companies to the
residence of Walter M. Bastian, the Washington D.C. district court judge. He scheduled an
emergency motion hearing for the issuance of a restraining order. The responsibility fell upon
Judge Alexander Holtzoff, who ruled denial of the motion on the grounds of balance of equities
favoring the government. In Holtzoff's written ruling, he considers damage to the plaintiff, citing
the fact that managerial staff were not displaced, but still possessed and conducted company
operations. He also states that an injunction levied on Sawyer would effectively be an injunction
levied on the President himself: Holtzoff believed this was something that should not be done
without vital reasoning.
Afterwards, the case was handed to Justice David Andrew Pine, hearing the motion for
preliminary injunction. Pine was most interested in the issue of whether the President had the
power itself to seize the facilities, while the companies were more focused on the equities and
the President’s power under the Tart-Hartley Act. However, once the issue of whether the
President could seize property without Congress approval was brought up, Pine asked for the
Assistant Attorney General to respond. He presented Article II of the Constitution, claiming the
President had inherent and implied powers associated with it, an extreme interpretation that the
administration itself did not align with. Truman would issue a statement afterwards that
acknowledged limitations the Constitution places upon the President’s power during national
emergencies. Soon after, Pine issued the injunction, strikes resumed, and the federal government
requested a stay order from Pine and appealed the case. The D.C. circuit court, sitting en banc,
granted the stay under the condition that the federal government petition for certiorari and would
last until SCOTUS acted on the petition. Upon doing so, the government found the steel
companies had already filed their own, and requested renewal for the stay.
SCOTUS shortly granted certiorari and issued a stay that stated the federal government
could not increase laborer wages during the pending appeal, but could maintain possession of the
steel mills post-seizure. This set the stage for oral argument in the Supreme Court ten days after
certiorari was granted, in which delegates of this committee will proceed to emulate and debate
on. The resource necessity of an ongoing war looms over this courtroom: each judge,
representative, and assistant of the court must tread carefully to reach an effective deliverance of
justice.

OLE ESCRIPTIONS
This committee includes select fictional characters and will be marked as such below. These
fictional characters are not named after nor based on any real people involved in the case.
Chief Justice Fred M. Vinson
Previously the Secretary of Treasury, a Congressman representing Kentucky, and an Associate
Justice for the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia, the Chief Justice led the court
with expertise. Vinson held moderately conservative views, generally favoring government
authority, flexibility, and national security over the topic of individual liberties. He often sought a
unified court that came to consensus, despite the ideological struggles between justices. He led
the “Vinson bloc,” a group of judges that voted together extremely frequently in non-unanimous
decisions: this included Vinson himself, Clark, Minton, Reed, and Burton.
Associate Justice Hugo L. Black
Justice Hugo Black was a liberal-leaning justice known for his absolutist interpretation of the
Constitution, tied to the beliefs of the era it had been written in: for example, he developed an
extremely absolutist interpretation of the First Amendment, and strictly interpreted the explicitly
granted powers to each branch of government. Black was previously involved in a dispute with
Justice Robert Jackson, but has since made amends after Vinson’s appointment as Chief Justice.
Associate Justice Stanley Reed
Justice Stanley Reed was a pragmatic New Deal Democrat, holding economically liberal and
socially conservative views. He frequently sided with liberal majorities in the court on most
issues related to economics, but clashed with issues surrounding civil rights. As such, he held a
centrist position that aligned with the court’s gradual shifting opinions on social liberalism. He
held a pragmatic, deferential interpretation stance of the Constitution, believing it must be
interpreted in light of real-world conditions and deference to other political branches.
16
Associate Justice Felix Frankfurter
Justice Felix Frankfurter was, despite being a New Deal appointed justice, a conservative justice
who championed the concept of judicial restraint: he believed the court must observe a limitation
of its own power, permitting existence of laws and government action that may be in violation of
the Constitution out of the principle of deferring to legislative bodies. In essence, he believed in a
powerful “separation of powers.” As such, he would often clash with other justices who sought
an opposite approach of judicial restraint.
Associate Justice William O. Douglas
Justice William Douglas was a strong, progressive civil libertarian, supporting the general idea of
individual rights against government overreach and interference. As a New Deal advocate,
Douglas also supported the federal government having greater power to regulate the national
economy. That meant when it came to the Constitution, Douglas held an opinion adjacent to that
of strict separation of powers, with a structural approach towards the text.
Associate Justice Robert H. Jackson
Justice Robert Jackson was a liberal, nationalistic, pragmatic justice who believed in the balance
of individual liberties with greater public order, working with a case-by-case approach that
rejected absolutist interpretations of the Constitution. Similar to other justices, he championed
distinct separation of powers, noting the necessity of differentiation in presidential power,
congressional authorization, and the relationship between the two. He is well known for his role
as chief prosecutor in the Nuremberg trials.
Associate Justice Harold Burton
Justice Harold Burton was a Republican justice who lacked a distinctive judicial philosophy, but
was a core advocate of judicial restraint, much like Frankfurter. Many of his decisions were
based on narrow procedural grounds rather than broader Constitutional merit, consisting of
things like consideration for jurisdiction and following specific legal procedure. His input could
help unify the court on procedural grounds when constitutional opinions were split.
17
Associate Justice Tom C. Clark
Justice Tom Clark was a pragmatic moderate who often functioned as a swing vote, with equal
usage of judicial restraint philosophy and more expansive constitutional interpretation. Notably,
his swinging moderate stance is made clear through his simultaneous support of constitutional
rights of criminal defendants alongside his general deference to the government on the
constitutionality of Cold War-era laws. Notably, he was previously the U.S. Attorney General,
consistent with his pro-government stance in some cases.
Associate Justice Sherman Minton
Justice Sherman Minton was another supporter of strong judicial restraint, deferring to the other
branches of government despite a New Deal liberal background and consistently voting liberal
during his Senate tenure. He would frequently side with the government over private citizen
matters. He favored the idea of minimizing judicial interference so long as it was not necessary.
However, similar to other justices with the same ideologies, he would vote in favor of civil rights
legislation.
John W. Davis
A former diplomat, House of Representatives member, and presidential candidate, John W. Davis
serves as the petitioner advocate in this case. Prior to being brought on to the case, Davis has
already provided an opinion to the Republic Steel Company on his stance against the President’s
implied power to seize the industry in event of a “national emergency.” A powerful presence in
the courtroom and admirable speaker to even justices, Davis’ extensive experience is made clear
through the 140 cases he argued in front of the Supreme Court. He championed conservative,
limited government ideas throughout his career.
Philip B. Perlman
Previously the Assistant Attorney General of Maryland and Secretary of State of Maryland,
Perlman was appointed as the Solicitor General under Truman. He holds moderately liberal
viewpoints, made evident through his civil rights cases taken while being a private lawyer, and
18
the legal approach of a strong advocate for his client, rather than that of an ideologue. He
believed in the utilization of government power to address social and private issues, reflective of
his argumentation in this case.
Arthur J. Goldberg
A future associate justice of the Supreme Court in the 1960s, Arthur J. Goldberg serves as an
amicus curiae for the United Steelworkers of America in this case. In the context of this case,
Goldberg still serves as general counsel to the same group. As such, his amicus curiae accounts
likely stem from his legal expertise, mirroring specific arguments made by the petitioner
advocate and serving to bolster their argument. Representing a labor union, he’ll likely seek a
favorable outcome for the workers themselves as well.
Clifford C. O’Brien
Speaking on behalf of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, Clifford C. O’Brien is an
amicus curiae in this case. He plays an important role in representing the interests of a labor
union, as this case carries broader implications beyond the privileges of the executive
surrounding the working privileges, rights, and results of the strikes that laborers initially went
on. As such, O’Brien likely argues with a positive outcome for the laborers themselves in mind.
Harold C. Heiss
Speaking on behalf of the Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, Harold C. Heiss plays an
identical role to that of O’Brien. The two will work together to represent the exclusive interests
of the labor union, ensuring that the decision and results of this case benefit the laborers and
union on top of the case reaching a favorable outcome for the petitioner.
Constance A. White (Fictional)
As a well-versed constitutional scholar and educated legal historian, Constance A. White serves
in this case as amicus curiae. White will likely be brought upon by the court to provide expert
opinion, evaluation, and input from an objective standpoint on constitutional interpretation and
involving precedent cases and their decisions, acting to advise justices to make a more
well-rounded consideration of the case.

Gordon Clarke (Fictional)
Serving as a defense official in the Truman administration, Gordon Clarke represents the
interests of the federal government and executive powers. His primary concerns surround the
resource necessity of the Korean War. While Clarke does not involve himself in this case with a

ensuring the U.S. military receives necessary resources like steel to advance the war effort.
Douglas Ward (Fictional)
As a representative given full independent operating authority for the General Dynamics Electric

legal principle consistency and the doctrine of stare decisis: standing by what’s decided already.

T P C
HE RIZE ASES
The Prize Cases were a set of four cases decided in 1863 surrounding circumstances of
the Civil War. In the wake of possible hostilities, Lincoln put in place a naval blockade around
the Confederate states, effectively deeming them belligerents, rather than insurrectionists or a
group the country would be at actual war with. In maritime law, capturing and impounding a ship
without a formally declared war was considered piracy, causing the cases to rise through courts
after merchants who had their ships and belongings seized by the U.S. Navy had claimed it was
legal to sell goods and wartime supplies to the Confederate states.
The Court proceeded to rule that the Confederate states were insurrectionists and were at
war with the Union, based on multiple acts of belligerency that had taken place a couple years
prior to the ruling. The court concluded that a war did not have to constitute conflict between two
independent nations, and that the presence of the Confederacy as a non-nation belligerent group
meant an ongoing war. What is important to note here is the reasoning behind the dissenting
opinion: the President is not given authority to declare war, but Congress is given the power to
by the Constitution. Lincoln had ordered the naval blockade before Congress had officially
declared war against the Confederacy. In the context of this committee, consider the implications
of the Prize Cases’ rulings and what they suggest about the distinctions between executive and
Congressional power.

IDEN V EBRASKA
In 2020, the campaigning presidential candidate Joe Biden promised to cancel student
debt for borrowers up to $10,000. After being elected to become the sitting president, the
administration announced their intent to carry out the same promise for any borrowers with
income less than $125,000 annually. A number of states, including Nebraska, challenged this and
21
claimed it violated the Administrative Procedure Act and separation of powers, with the
administration claiming that under the Higher Education Relief Opportunities for Students
(HEROES) Act, the Secretary of Education had the authority to cancel said debt.
The court ruled that the HEROES Act did not permit this, as it only permits the Secretary
to modify statutory provisions in a minor way. The cancellation of billions of dollars worth of
debt would be a change that fundamentally altered how Congress’s approved loan forgiveness
program would have functioned. Another key reason for the rejection was the claim that the
nature of the COVID-19 pandemic justified the debt cancellation plan. In the context of this
committee, consider how reasoning was attempted to be used here in the context of the pandemic
and how that might align with other national emergencies and times of crisis and how the
circumstances surrounding Congressional and executive authority tie back as well.
