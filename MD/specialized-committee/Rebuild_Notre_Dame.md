Baroque style - A cultural movement in the 1600s, which highlights elaborate details such as

1100-1500. It has prominent features such as pointed arches and incredibly tall buildings.
Grandeur - A quality or state of something being grand, magnificent, or impressive.
Vault - An architectural feature which helps to support the ceiling or roof of a building.
A human chain/‘Bucket Brigade’ - A method used to transport items from one place to another
using individuals who are stationary, and passing it to one another. This was used in the form of
passing buckets of water in the firefighting effort seen.
Short circuit - An electrical mechanism which allows for electric current to flow through an
unintended path with low resistance, where the resistance is usually much greater. This can cause
fires to begin.

ISTORICAL ACKGROUND
The Kingdom of the Franks (which was a precursor to France) traditionally followed a Pagan
tradition, until in the fourth century AD, when King Clovis, who was King of the Franks and was
the religious and political founder of the Kingdom, converted the Kingdom to Christianity. This
began the era of Paris becoming a Christian centre, and solidifying the city as a place for the
Christian faith in Europe.
With this development and growth of population, a need for a place of worship grew in order to
have a community. This led to the creation of the first cathedral in Paris, Saint-Étienne. The
Cathedral of Saint-Étienne was built on the Île de la Cité (Island at the heart of Paris) and was a
precursor to the construction of the Notre-Dame. The Île de la Cité was not only important due to
the establishment of Saint-Étienne, but it was also a political centre of French politics. The Palais
de la Cité was where kings resided for many years. This location was pivotal, and the
establishment of the cathedral was a success.
Over the next few centuries, the Diocese of Paris had flourished especially under Saint Marcel
and Saint Germain. However, the Cathedral of Saint-Étienne was no longer able to support this
growth, and there became an increasing need to upsize in relation to the grandeur that Paris was
now receiving, especially with the prominence of the Virgin Mary that Parisians focused upon. It
was in the twelfth century when Bishop Maurice de Sully, who had witnessed the Basilica of
Saint-Denis and the ever-growing influence of Gothic architecture, envisioned something bigger:
Notre-Dame de Paris. In 1163, the first stone was laid for this project, with Pope Alexander III
gracing the ceremony. This was the beginning of a journey, as it was not until two centuries later
that the building of the Notre-Dame de Paris was completed and remained important. The
building of the Notre-Dame Cathedral occurred on the original site of Saint-Étienne Cathedral,
and so the original foot traffic for the Cathedral was included in the opening of the brand new
place of worship.

By the 17th century, Notre-Dame and France gained prestige when Louis XIV dedicated France
to the Virgin Mary. Due to the name of the Cathedral translating to ‘Our Lady of Paris’ this was a
monumental step, as it showcased the Notre-Dame Cathedral and Paris as fundamental to the
14
country as a whole. This gained prestige meant that renovations became necessary on the
Notre-Dame to model it after the increasingly popular Baroque style, so that it remained as a
pivotal landmark and place of worship. Under architect Robert de Cotte, the choir was
redesigned, and medieval elements were removed such as the stained glass windows. This was to
reflect the taste and move into contemporary elements, which was a large theme at the time
considering the arrival of the French Revolution.
The French Revolution occurred due to public unease over the display of aristocratic and royal
grandeur at the Notre-Dame, as it coincided with a time when the French people were starving.
In 1793, the Notre-Dame Cathedral was ransacked, which left many of the treasures found within
the Cathedral destroyed. In addition to the Notre-Dame de Paris being ransacked, the banning of
Catholicism occurred. As the Notre-Dame could no longer serve its purpose as a place for
Catholic worship, it was repurposed as a ‘Temple of Reason’ and a wine warehouse. This
repurposing of the Notre-Dame continued up until the nineteenth century, where the prior
grandeur that it had was entirely lost.
The revival of the Notre-Dame occurred in an unconventional way, as it was the creation of the
novel ‘Notre-Dame de Paris’ (The English title: The Hunchback of the Notre-Dame) by Victor
Hugo. Hugo’s admiration for the Notre-Dame led to this novel being completed in 1831, and just
thirteen years later, the architect Eugène Viollet-le-Duc started a twenty-five-year restoration
project. This restoration project restored some of the Gothic elements, especially the wooden
spire that was added. This was the same spire that burnt down in 2019. This brought back the
grandeur that the Notre-Dame de Paris once had, and led to UNESCO declaring it a World
Heritage Site a century later in 1991.

URRENT EVENTS
On the 15th April 2019, at around 18:20pm a fire alarm was triggered and led to the evacuation
of the Cathedral and a guard was sent to investigate. There was a considerable amount of
confusion from the staff, which led to the guard checking the adjacent sacristy instead of the
actual attic in the Notre-Dame (La Forêt). It wasn’t until half an hour later that the mistake was
discovered, and once the correct location was searched, the fire had already spread through the
wooden lattice structure that was holding up the Cathedral’s lead roof.
Despite the initial confusion surrounding the location of the fire, firefighters emerged at the
scene by 19:00pm CEST. When the firefighters began to climb the three hundred steps up to the
attic, they were pushed back by the fire and were unable to save the attic due to the safety
concerns for the firefighters. There was a large firefighting force of approximately five hundred,
and leading the tactical effort was the Deputy Commander of the Paris Fire Brigade who was
able to direct their efforts towards an important consideration: minimising damage.
In order to ensure the entirety of the Notre-Dame was not burnt down, the firefighting efforts
were directed at the Cathedral’s towers. There was a great deal of fear that if the Northern tower
(which had already caught on fire) was not extinguished, it would lead to the collapse of eight
bells which in turn would have destroyed the rest of the Cathedral. Fortunately, utilising methods
such as the ‘Bucket Brigade’ human chain, they were able to avoid the worst-case scenario and
localise the damage of the fire to the attic and roof. By 21:45 that evening, most of the fire had
been extinguished, and by the early hours on the 16th of April, the fire was fully extinguished
which put an end to the ordeal.

There were no reported casualties, injuries, or deaths, and some of the Cathedral’s iconic features
such as the famed rose windows remained intact. However, by 10am on the 16th of April, the
world had changed.
When trying to assess as to exactly what had gone wrong, and why the fire was able to spread as
far as it had, there was an initial investigation made. There were several factors which led to the
intensity of the fire increasing, the first of which was the lack of sprinklers in the Notre-Dame’s
ceiling. This meant that when the fire had been detected, the only option to stop the fire was to
rely on the ‘Bucket Brigade/Human Chain’ method of firefighting, which certainly slowed down
the effort. Additionally, the Cathedral in the year 2018 had treated an issue with the timber in the
cathedral with highly flammable antifungal spray, which led to the fire being able to spread much
faster than had alternatives been pursued.
Finally, logistical issues when putting out the fire had a large impact on the extent in which the
damage was spread. The water pressure from the fire hoses was poor and could not appropriately

deal with the flame. The firefighters themselves due to the issue of narrow passages in the

surrounding this event, the Notre-Dame de Paris was not well-equipped with fire prevention, but
rather a harsh lesson was learnt that prevention was not a priority, and that disaster management
became a necessity.
The population of France is mourning the damage to an important landmark, and there has been

conclusion would be. Many leaders of different countries, religious leaders, and individuals are
all expressing messages of sympathy. However, there is a distinct undertone of confusion, as
there is no clear answer yet as to how this happened. Was this purposeful? Did something go
wrong with the electrical works within the Cathedral? These are all questions that have not been
made clear yet with the information given by the time the committee is meeting.
All eyes are on the advisory body to act. What will you do?

ASE TUDY HE ESTORATION OF T AUL S ATHEDRAL IN ONDON
The Burning of the Notre-Dame de Paris was a unique event which had unique circumstances.
However, this is not the first time in history in which an important building faced unexpected
damage. After the events of the Blitz in London during WWII, St Paul’s Cathedral in London
suffered two direct bomb hits over the course of many months, as well as many other dangers.
The direct bomb hits had lifted the quire roof from the Cathedral, which caused the masonry to
fall and this destroyed the altar below it. As well as the second bomb hit in 1941, rocking the
dome, exposing the crypt below the floor, and the pushing of the south wall of the South transept
which broke every window.
St Paul’s has a similar level of history and prestige as the Notre-Dame de Paris, and once the
environment was safe again, there was discourse around how to restore the Cathedral to its
former glory and ensure continued tourism to London. As the bombs that had hit St Paul’s
Cathedral presented a fire risk, teams of volunteers worked hard over the course of months to
ensure there were no fire risks for the sake of the building, any damage that was inflicted onto
the Cathedral was restored by architects, and that there was little danger to surrounding buildings
and locals, there was a decision made. Three of those who had assisted and risked their lives
during these events were presented with the George Cross for their effort and bravery, whilst
others were taken out by the locals for a drink as a thank you.
Alongside recognising the courage and bravery of those involved, there were decisions made on
what to do next regarding the restoration process. Below is a document highlighting a portion of
the process. A Letter to Dean Walter Matthews, from members of the St Paul's Watch, 11
October 1942, to mark their contribution towards the replacement of the keystone to the arch
over the High Altar (Ref. No. SPCAA/SPW/3/6/3).
19
Those who were discussing this case as showcased above discussed the appropriate material, and
justifying the cost due to ‘it looking down on the faithful for many centuries to come’. The
request of donations in order to complete this project was an integral part of this project, as the
materials needed to ensure that St Paul’s Cathedral would need to be sturdy, appropriate, and
able to withstand any future calamities if they arose. Funding was not only given from generous
donations from the public, but also from other funding such as the War Damage Commission.
20
Key elements such as the vaulting, carvings, mosaics, and full repair of the North Transept were
completed over time, with the assistance of individuals who understood the cultural, historical,
and material considerations. Those who made the decisions focused their efforts to ensure that
the restoration was replicating the work that had previously been there, whilst working with
considerable constraints due to the country being actively in war and then recovering from that
war economically and emotionally.
Most of the major restoration work was completed by 1946, just a few years after the two direct
bomb hits which caused that level of damage. In 1948, the King and Queen of the United
Kingdom were able to host their Silver Anniversary Service in the Cathedral. Upholding the
tradition behind it, and showcasing that whilst the Cathedral may not have been fully restored,
some normality could arise during this time. The full restoration of St Paul’s Cathedral was
completed in 1962, and to this day stands strong in the Heart of London. It continues to serve the
religious community, bolster tourism which encourages individuals to learn the history and
culture behind the piece, and is an iconic piece of English history.
This case study showcases despite this disaster occurring many decades prior, it has many
similarities and things to consider when implementing the plan. Will you mimic this plan with
rewarding the efforts of those who were brave, seek fundraising methods, look into appropriate
materials and designs, and have a timeframe to complete the work in? It is wise to look into this
case study for both inspiration and ways to improve, as well as considering modern elements
such as the rise of technology and social media. The damage to St Paul’s Cathedral was on a
smaller scale, so ensuring appropriate effort, funds, and timing is given will be crucial to
answering this.
