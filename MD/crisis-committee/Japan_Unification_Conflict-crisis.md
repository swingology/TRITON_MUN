EY ERMS
- Daimyō
- Powerful and influential Japanese lords, typically vassals of the shogun. Defined
through their control over large areas of lands and its peoples. Typically, they
commanded samurai armies, with leadership of their armies and land being passed
down heretically. At the time of 1560, due to political discourse, many of the
Daimyō were independent of the Shogun.
- Shogun
- The centralized military leader of Japan, passed down from generation to
generation through the same family. Though the emperor does exist due to the
weakness and actual power of the emperor, the Shogun is essentially the leader of
Japan. At the time of 1560, the Shogun had devolved into a position controlled
externally and was nothing more than a figurehead who had little to no actual
power, this being the Ashikaga shogunate.
- shi-nō-kō-shō system
- Though not officially put into place until the 1600s, the system still refers to the
developing caste system that separated roles of individuals in Japanese society.
From the top to the bottom were warriors and rulers, farmers, artisans, and finally
the lowest merchants. During the time of this committee, this system was based
more so on merit and influence rather than heredity, thus those in the merchant
class could often still be considered in the highest class of rulers based on ability
and merit.
- Ashigaru
The light-weight common foot soldiers often being farmers conscripted into
battle, serving their respected samurai. Making up a majority of armies and being
lightly armored and armed with weapons such as spears, bows, and in some cases
firearms.
- Samurai
- The military caste of Japan, typically being highly skilled and trained warriors
being retainers to a respective Daimyō. Essentially Samurai were the elites
10
making up Daimyō's army making up a much smaller percentage of armies
though playing just a large role.
- Kumi
- Groups of small organized and specialized troops in an army, each using one
particular type of weapon. They would utilize weapons such as pikes, arquebuses,
and bows.
- Koku system
- The way used to assess the prosperity of a region and in turn taxes, stipends for
samurai, and wealth of the Daimyō who controlled the region. This was entirely
based around how much rice a region could produce, one koku being equivalent
to 180 liters/150 kg of rice being able to be produced. Higher values meaning
more power and thus higher taxes
- Arquebus
- A portable matchlock and smoothbore firearm introduced by the Portuguese and
reverse-engineered in 1543. The arquebus could only be used in the hundreds and
still see a huge difference in effectiveness in combat.
- Sonae
- Units of troops consistent of anywhere between 300 to 800+ troops loyal to a
specific Daimyō. Each functioned as an independent unit however led by a
retainer to the Daimyō and consisting of a variety of Kumi, samurai, and cavalry.
- Clans
- Independent military units typically led by a singular family with leadership
(being the Daimyō) being passed down from generation to generation. These units
would lead armies following the will of the current Daimyō they follow. The
structure was defined by the head being the Daimyō and beneath them would be
Samurai retainers who would typically be family, heirs, and enemies who
submitted to the Daimyō (each would typically lead a Sonae).
- Jizamurai
- Translating to samurai of the land, these were the individuals who bridged the gap
between the leading and farming class. These individuals functioned as smaller
11
local leaders and landowners. They serve a crucial role in control of a region as
each holds some sort of influence over a given piece of land.
- Yamashiro province
- The province which contained the capital of Japan at the time. This province was
extremely important being centralized and control of this province translating into
the control over the shogun and in turn Japan. Thus control over this province was
critical to any Daimyō hoping to lead the country, hence the province being
defined by constant conflict and chaos.
- Hirajiro/yamajiro
- Different types of castles or fortresses built upon flatlands or mountains
respectively. Each of these served as critical tactical points improving trade
logistics, and protection over regions. In short these castles were essential pieces
of logistics and military operations, determining effective control over a region.
- Tanada
- Agricultural terraces which were usually dedicated towards the growth of rice.
These served an imperative role in the prosperity of a region and being able to
feed an army. These terraces also directly influenced the Koku of a region and
thus prosperity. Essentially the more Tanada a region had the more prosperous it
typically was.
- Bunkokuhō
- Domain or provincial laws enacted by an individual region’s Daimyō. Put in place
after the fall of the Ashikaga shogunate, they are individualized to every region
based on the whims of their Daimyō. They differed greatly ranging from laws to
control farmers or retainers, to taxes and whole law systems unique to regions
controlled by that Daimyō.

ISTORICAL ACKGROUND
In 1467, the Ōnin War began, resulting in the collapse of the power of the Ashikaga
shogunate, meaning that there was no longer a shogun and thus central organization of military
power, transforming the shogunate into a powerless figurehead. Ultimately, this split the empire
into regions controlled by Daimyō whom the shogunate lost control of as a result of the war.
Normally, this would not be a problem, however given the caste system forming in Japan at the
time, the shi-nō-kō-shō system, Daimyō now essentially became regional Lords whose word was
law (known as Bunkokuhō). The Ōnin War would set the tone for the years coming up to 1560,
seeing hundreds of wars and thousands more injured or killed due to the fracturing of the nation.
During this time period, many would rise to prominence just to as quickly lose it all. Though at
the time, the shi-nō-kō-shō system would become a widely accepted system, this era saw
unprecedented mobility through the system with many of those in lower castes such as farmers
or merchants being able to rise to become powerful and notable warriors and rulers.
Given the enticing opportunity of unifying and leading Japan, many would consolidate
power in their home and surrounding regions, often clashing with neighboring Daimyō. This
resulted in years of small territorial wars in the aims of expanding territory and gaining an upper
hand in the ultimate struggle of ruling Japan. During this time, warfare was heavily reliant on
cavalry, archery, and pikes as counters to cavalry, with typically the victor being decided through
sheer numbers. During these years, warfare would remain relatively stagnant until the
introduction of guns in 1543 on the island of Tanegashima through Portuguese traders
(Goldman). The guns from the supposedly wrecked and crashed Portuguese ship would be
reverse-engineered and produced across the island. The introduction of guns into warfare was
slow though impactful, slowly evolving warfare from an emphasis on archers to troops armed
with arquebuses. However, firearms would not be the only change, as six years later with the
arrival of Francis Xavier came the introduction of Christianity into Japan (“History of
Christianity in Japan”). Christianity did not stand simply as a new rival religion to the dominant
Shinto-Buddhist religion, rather alongside this the introduction of Christianity instilled fear of an
inevitable European conquest into many at the time.
13
Wars would rage on without there being a clear candidate for unifying Japan, with the
shogunate continuing to be simply a puppet and figurehead. During this time there would be
much political discourse within the capital with an internal struggle breaking out resulting in the
upheaval of the Ashikaga shogunate. The current shogun Ashikaga Yoshiteru would not truly
return until around 1559, spending nine years in exile (Ronald K). With regions being won and
lost at a whim, one Daimyō stood out, Imagawa Yoshimoto, who in the summer of 1560 formed
an unprecedented three-way alliance with the powerful Takeda and Hōjō clans. With this alliance
securing his rear, Yoshimoto amassed an army of anywhere between 25,000 to 40,000 troops
beginning his march to the capital to secure his control over the capital and all the land
inbetween. Between Yoshimoto and the capital stood the land controlled by the Oda clan,
however Yoshimoto was not worried due to his numbers advantage and the fear his newly
amassed army caused. After many crushing victories against the Oda clan, it seemed almost
certain of Yoshimoto’s control over the capital and thus leading of the country. However, this
would come to change after Oda Nobunaga led a surprise attack against the Imagawa forces
whilst they were resting at Dengakuhazama Gorge. The attack resulted in the death of Imagawa
Yoshimoto and the dissolution of his army to a point where they could not recover. This upset
secured Nobunaga the Owari province once more and further plunged Japan into a state of chaos
with no clear candidate for ruling the country. Alongside this chaos emerged a newly determined
Nobunaga, and similarly many other Daimyo now making plans to rule Japan.

Battle of Okehazama
A pivotal battle in the Sengoku Jidai period which solidified Oda Nobunaga as a
prominent Daimyo and highlighted the importance of tactics and knowledge in battle. The battle
started due to the powerful Daimyo Imagawa Yoshimoto securing his Eastern border through the
marriages of his sister and son into the powerful Takeda and Hōjō clans respectively (“The Battle
of Okehazama”). With his two biggest rivals in a three-way alliance, Imagawa Yoshimoto
decided it was the best time to mobilize his army of at least 25,000 in Kyoto and overthrow the
Ashikaga shogunate. Between him and Kyoto stood the Oda-controlled Owari province and Oda
forces whom Yoshimoto outnumbered more than several times. Expecting an easy victory,
Yoshimoto marched upon the Owari province.
As expected, the Imagawa forces saw little to no difficulty throughout their campaign.
With several fortresses and key strategic points falling under Yoshimoto’s control and the
dwindling morale and resources of the Oda forces, Yoshimoto’s victory was almost certain. Thus
with victory in sight, the Imagawa forces on their way to Odaka castle decided to rest and
celebrate their recent victories at Okehazamayama, specifically in the Dengakuhazama gorge.
Meanwhile, the battered Oda forces numbering around 2,000 led by Oda Nobunaga decided that
instead of simply attempting to defend a fortress and inevitably lose, they would lead a direct
assault upon the Imagawa forces. Recognizing their numbers disadvantage, Nobunaga played
into their strengths being their knowledge of Dengakuhazma gorge and a misdirection in tactics.
To further their element of surprise, Oda forces raised fake banners and planted decoys at
Zensho-ji Fort confusing Yoshimoto’s reconnaissance. Finally under the cover of a violent
rainstorm, the Oda forces began their attack on Imagawa forces by utilizing passages through
mountains and valleys. Given the combined factors of Imagawa forces celebrating, a
misinformed army, and the Oda forces' unexpected entrance into the gorge the Imagawa forces
were taken completely off guard, barely being able to raise a defense. The sudden attack and
death of Imagawa Yoshimoto by Mouri Shinsuke led to the retreat of Imagawa forces and
complete victory for the Oda Nobunaga (“About the Battle of Okehazama”). Afterwards, Oda
forces would practically eliminate most of the higher powers of the Imagawa forces with the rest
submitting to Nobunaga.
15
With the death of Imagawa Yoshimoto and the destruction of his army there came several
outcomes. First was the end of the alliance between the rival clans of Imagawa, Takeda, and
Hōjō. The end of this alliance, meant a rocky and uneasy relationship between the Takeda and
Hōjō clans. This once again locked prominent Daimyo in a stalemate. Furthermore, alongside the
weakening of the Imagawa was the release of Matsudaira Motoyasu later known as Tokugawa
Ieyasu, who would proceed to become an independent Daimyo securing the Mikawa province
and making a name for himself given his political ability (“Ieyasu and the Unification of
Mikawa”). Finally was the unexpected victory of the Oda forces over Yoshimoto. This total
victory gave Nobunaga the momentum to retake his home province of Owari, and national
recognition as a major Daimyo (Kimball). In short, the Battle of Okehazama was a major turning
point in the Sengoku Jidai which showcased the importance of tactics over numbers, and just
how rapidly situations could change during the Sengoku Jidai.
Ōnin War
By 1467, the power of the Ashikaga shogunate had declined to a point of weakness that a
simple succession issue would lead to chaos. The shogunate lost power due to consolidating
within the capital and appointing many individuals control over land outside the capital.
Additionally, Ashikaga Yoshimasa, the shogun at the time, was considered a weak leader who
prioritized art and culture, resulting in the ensuing succession. Thus, when a dispute between his
brother and own son started over who would succeed Yoshimasa, the capital fell into chaos. The
conflict became a problem however when Yamana Sōzen and his son-in-law, Hosokawa
Katsumoto, who controlled armies of 160,000 and 110,000 respectively, sided with differing
factions in the succession dispute (Turnbull). The actual fighting started due to the Hosokawa
attacking one of the mansions of a Yamana general, utilizing swords, arrows, and fire, which set
the stage for the conflict. Each side would be protecting and backing one of the possible next
Shogun in their respective mansions in the capital. This would lead to an 11-year conflict that
only evolved as time went on, known as the Ōnin War.
Though the Ōnin War started with a small political dispute, it would ultimately have
lasting effects on the capital, the political system of Japan, and warfare, and led to the conditions
seen in the Sengoku Jidai. As previously stated, swords, arrows, and fire were used, however as
time progressed, weapons would not just be limited to these. Eventually, due to contact with
16
China, weapons such as exploding arrows and fire spears started appearing. The combined usage
of fire alongside these weapons would see the capital devastated from the various fires, street
warfare, and eventually looters who pillaged the city (Matthew). Ultimately, this left the once
vibrant and culturally rich Kyōto nothing more than charred ruins. As many of those in the
centralized government lived and operated out of the capital, this would leave the country
without a real government, with leadership instead falling to those appointed land prior to the
Ōnin War.
The Ōnin War was the start of the Sengoku Jidai and highlighted several key factors that
would be prevalent throughout the era, mainly revolving around the actual power of the
shogunate and thus a centralized government. As seen from the beginning of the conflict, the title
of Shogun held enough power to start an 11-year-long war which saw the destruction of Kyōto
and the lives of those who lived in and around the city. Furthermore, the Ōnin War is the whole
reason for the Sengoku Jidai starting to showcase the ambitions of the Daimyo and the lengths
many were willing to go in order to control the military-based government of Japan. The final
factor is the recurring theme of control over the shogunate and capital to rule Japan, versus the
leadership falling to the in many cases more capable Daimyo. In short, the Ōnin War not only
started the Sengoku Jidai but remained relevant throughout the years of conflicts for these
reasons, and forms of warfare.

HARACTER ROFILES
Oda Nobunaga
A great warrior, politician, and strategic genius. At the time of 1560, Nobunaga was one
of the most prominent Daimyō, after his victory over Imagawa Yoshimoto (who was expected to
become the next shogun). As of this time, Nobunaga really only controls the Owari region
though he is not content with simply securing his region's borders. Nobunaga uniquely
incorporates firearms and unique strategies into warfare which has led to his overwhelming
victories and his enemies fearing him. Furthermore, he has gained a lot of momentum and
prominence after his surprising victory over Imagawa Yoshimoto. Nobunaga ultimately aims to
unify Japan through force and bring about a new era.
Maeda Toshimasu
Also known as Maeda Keiji and often referred to as the greatest samurai gangster.
Toshimasu was a legendary warrior known for his unparalleled strength and size. Toshimasu was
extremely close with his loyal and one-of-a-kind horse, Matsukaze, with their bond and strength
being known across Japan. Though not in a major position of leadership, Toshimasu remains a
legendary warrior who was highly educated, independent, and culturally knowledgeable.
Toshimasu does not control land outside of Noto province, though he has potential to govern
much more. Toshimasu values personal freedom and expression more than anything else with
politics not being a priority for him.
Takeda Shingen
Known as the Tiger of Kai, Shingen is a master tactician who was highly educated and
highly renowned for his strategy which at the time not many could truly compare to. As of 1560,
Shingen controlled his home province of Kai, Shinano province, and parts of the Kozuke
Province. Shingen was known for his knowledge of espionage, communication, and his
devastating cavalry-based strategies. Shingen not only is a genius tactician but also a master of
civil administration and infrastructure, which would see regions under his control having actual
fair laws and well-built infrastructure. Like many, Shingen wishes to unify Japan, wanting to
bring order back to the shogunate and Japan in general.
18
Uesugi Kenshin
The Dragon of Echigo, Kenshin was a genius strategist whose skill in tactics were said to
only be rivaled by Takeda Shingen. Kenshin controls four main locations: his main province of
Echigo, Kantō Region, parts of the Kōzuke Province, and Northern Shinano. However, unlike
others, Daimyō Kenshin has little to no ambition in terms of expanding his territory, rather often
only involving himself in conflicts for honorable or moral reasons. Kenshin was not only known
for his skill in strategy but also a skillful administrator, which ultimately saw a higher standard of
living in his regions. Finally, Kenshin was considered a great figure in the eyes of Buddhists,
often being called the incarnation of the Buddhist god of war Bishamonten.
Tokugawa Ieyasu
Heir to the Matsudaira clan and a master politician known for his patience and ability to
navigate the political world of Japan. Though as of now he only controls his home province of
Mikawa, he has many allies and loyal retainers whom he can call upon. One such as Hattori
Hanzo, an extremely talented ninja, samurai and leader of the Iga ninja. Ieyasu is known for his
patience and political skill, often not taking risks and instead waiting for the right time to make a
move. Through his political skill, he is easily able to make alliances with many and ultimately
lead to his uncanny ability to survive dire situations. After his recent release from the Imagawa
clan, his opportunities are endless, seeing how quickly he was able to unify his home province.
Ieyasu aimed to unify Japan and, in turn, create a long-lasting stabilized government, securing
his family’s legacy.
Hōjō Ujiyasu
A master of defensive warfare and abnormal battle tactics, Ujiyasu, also known as Lion
of Sagami, was a legendary figure standing among other great Daimyō such as Takeda Shingen
and Uesugi Kenshin. Ujiyasu controls five provinces: Sagami, Izu, Musashi, Shimōsa, and
Kazusa, though is often involved in conflict resulting in constant defensive battles. Ujiyasu not
only was well-versed in combat but also knowledgeable in diplomacy, governance, and culture,
implementing the Hōjō Regulations, and making alliances and truces to benefit himself. Ujiyasu
did not have much of the same ambition as the other Daimyō, rather than wishing to expand and
unify Japan Ujiyasu preferred stability and prosperity of regions he already controlled.
19
Akechi Mitsuhide
Mitsuhide stands out among many others through having a very obscure and minor
background, however, this did not stop his ability to rapidly gain power in a relatively short
timespan. Mitsuhide was a cunning and extremely intelligent samurai known for his deep
intellectual and cultural knowledge, and ability in governance and regional administration.
Although Mitsuhide currently controls only the Tanba Province and parts of Omi Province, he
should not be underestimated as these regions are a part of central Japan and thus play a major
factor in deciding the shogun. Mitsuhide strives for a peaceful and stable Japan, wanting to end
the chaotic times of the Sengoku Jidai, backing the traditional Ashikaga shogunate, and restoring
their power.
Ōtomo Sōrin
The progressive Daimyō, Sōrin was known for adopting several foreign policies and
technologies into his controlled provinces. Known for being one of the only Daimyō who
converted to Catholicism, Sōrin not only encouraged Christianity but also adopted many Western
technologies into his territories, hoping in the end to see a Christian kingdom. His
progressiveness also led to an openness to international trade, which led to decent relations with
foreign powers and the usage of firearms in his own military. After the battle of Okehazama,
Sōrin mainly controlled Bungo, Buzen, Chikuzen, and Chikugo, though had influence in Higo,
Hizen, and Iyo, controlling a good chunk of northern Japan. Sōrin wanted to see a unified
Christian Japan, which would see many European principles governing it, alongside foreign
trade.
Toyotomi Hideyoshi
Advancing from being a simple farmer to a capable politician, strategist, and warrior,
Hideyoshi is one of the most unique of the era. Even though he did not control much territory
after 1560, outside of fortresses like Nagahama Castle, which was built in a major transport hub,
Hideyoshi makes up for this through his numerous other skills. Hideyoshi is an expert politician
known for his ability to turn even the most loyal retainers against their lords. Furthermore,
Hideyoshi was also known for his ability in siege warfare and rapid logistic response. Hideyoshi
aims for a unified Japan strong enough to conquer foreign powers and expand borders, whilst
being unable to challenge his authority.
20
Mōri Motonari
Coming from relatively humble origins, Mōri Motonari has risen to prominence as a very
powerful daimyō. Originally the second in line to the Mōri samurai family, the death of
Motonari’s older brother allowed him to secure power in the family, and he soon launched
conquests against several other families to eventually seize control over a large swath of territory
across 10 different provinces. By 1560, Motonari was renowned for his cunning and strategic
brilliance, and his three sons were loyal and similarly talented leaders within their father’s
territory. Beyond his military capabilities, Motonari was also an exceptionally talented diplomat
and poet, and he is one of the few daimyō who could reasonably stand a chance against Oda
Nobunaga’s expansionism.
Kobayakawa Takakage
The third and youngest of Mōri Motonari’s sons was originally born Tokujumaru before
eventually becoming heir to the Kobayakawa family, taking the name Takakage. When he first
took power, the Kobayakawa family was divided into two sects, but his marriage in 1551 helped
to bridge this rift, securing him at its head. Although relatively untested as an independent leader
and general, he served under his father as a naval commander and played a significant role in his
victory in the Battle of Miyajima in 1555, which ultimately allowed the family to consolidate
much of their territories. Now, he’s hungry to prove himself and begin his own pursuits beyond
working with his father.
Kennyo
Kennyo Kōsa is one of the main leaders of the Ikko-Ikki, a collection of radical
Hongan-ji Buddhist fanatics. The group was originally founded as a religious sect, but over the
years, it has become immensely popular among peasants and gained a significant following.
Now based in the Ishiyama Hongan-ji fortress, the Ikko-Ikki has allies in clans and groups across
the island, such as clan Asakura and the Ashikaga shogunate. However, its growing military
force has drawn the attention of warlords seeking to eliminate potential threats to their rule.
While Kennyo and his forces of warrior monks have largely kept to themselves recently, they
have the potential to begin expanding their territory should they choose to do so.
21
Chōsokabe Motochika
Based in his stronghold of Oko Castle, Chōsokabe Motochika is the leader of the
Chōsokabe clan and has steadily been on the rise. In May 1560, he recently won one of his first
battles as a leader in the Battle of Tonomoto Nagahama, claiming Nagahama Castle alongside his
father, Kunichika. Until recently, there have been many questions as to whether or not he can
serve as an effective wartime leader, but the young daimyō has finally managed to gain some
momentum coming into the impending conflict. Now controlling much of Shikoku, Motochika
could be a valuable ally for any warlords seeking to mount an offensive, assuming that he
doesn’t seek to begin a conquest of his own on the mainland.
Ashikaga Yoshiteru
As Shogun of the Ashikaga shogunate, Ashikaga Yoshiteru rules the remains of what
was, at one point, one of Japan’s strongest powers. However, the remains of the shogunate have
since declined to a shadow of its former self, with Yoshiteru sequestering himself in Shōryūji
Castle in the small Yamashiro Province. However, a new opportunity has presented itself to
Yoshiteru, as he has recently approved the presence of Father Gaspar Vilela, a Portuguese priest
and missionary. While he has not actively provided aid for any of his missions, Yoshiteru has
thus proved himself a potential ally for Portugal by allowing Vilela to continue his work. So
though his military may currently be diminished, Yoshiteru’s political potential is far from
obsolete.
Miyoshi Nagayoshi
Miyoshi Nagayoshi has fought long and hard to grow his clan to new heights. Nagayoshi
has taken his clan from a very small territory to now controlling the majority of the Kinai region,
including Kyoto itself. Nagayoshi has proven himself a highly capable military strategist and
politician, weathering sieges and forming alliances to maximize his power with the goal of
unifying Japan under his rule. While his power is not absolute, he has taken enough steps to
secure important political alliances and protection from the prospect of an uprising, especially
with the Muromachi shogunate, which remains present in Kyoto under Nagayoshi’s rule.
Recently, he moved his base of operations to Iimoriyama Castle in the mountains of Osaka,
highly fortified and ready for whatever conflict is to come.
22
Amago Haruhisa
Amago Haruhisa took over as daimyō and head of the Amago clan following the
retirement of his grandfather. As part of the territory he took over, he rallied his military forces
throughout the San’in region to expand his territory into the Sanyo province, as well. His most
recent conquest came against the Shingu clan, led by his uncle, Amago Kunihisa, and he
absorbed the remnants into his own forces. Haruhisa is a ruthless warrior and keen strategist
seeking to strengthen his forces and secure his political power. However, expansion requires
funds, and Haruhisa has had his eye on the Omori Silver Mine for some time now. If his past is
any indication, he will not hesitate to make moves to expand.
Honda Tadakatsu
As one of the youngest warriors of his time, Honda Tadakatsu has already established
himself as a samurai prodigy. The son of Honda Tadamasa and heir to the Honda clan, he has a
clear path laid out for him: continue serving with his father, learn to govern the land, and
eventually succeed him when the time comes. Currently, the Honda clan has committed itself to
serving Tokugawa Ieyasu and his mission, and so far, their interests have been aligned in seeking
security through expansion. However, Tadakatsu is already proving himself a talented general
and warrior, and it may only be a matter of time before he decides to set out on his own with his
loyal clan beside him.
Asakura Yoshikage
Unlike many of the other daimyō of his time, Asakura Yoshikage has little public interest
in ruthlessly expanding his territory. As head of the Asakura clan, he has sought to ensure peace
and prosperity from the beginning, ending the feud between his people and the Ikko-Ikki as soon
as he came to power. He has since chosen to serve as a diplomatic and political leader from his
home in Ichijōdani Castle. Yoshikage’s reign over the Echizen province has been characterized
by rapid social and cultural development, and his military leaders and generals have done well to
keep his reign secure. One of his next big goals is to expand trade, and he largely opposes
expansion, but he will do what it takes to keep his people safe.
