# Position Paper

**Country:** Madagascar

**Delegate:** MB

**Committee:** UNESCO — Linguistic and Cultural Diversity in a Globalized World

**Topic:** The Preservation of Linguistic and Cultural Diversity in the Face of Globalization

---

## Background

Madagascar occupies a singular place in the global story of linguistic history. Unlike most nations whose languages descend from the same continental region they inhabit, Malagasy — the national language of Madagascar — is Austronesian in origin, most closely related to the Ma'anyan language of Borneo. This linguistic identity is the inheritance of seafaring settlers who reached the island roughly 1,500 years ago, and it represents one of the most remarkable migrations in human prehistory. The Malagasy language is therefore not merely a tool of communication — it is living evidence of an ancient voyage, a cultural artifact of extraordinary depth. Today, that heritage is under threat.

Long before French colonization, Madagascar experienced its own internal linguistic stratification. The Kingdom of Merina, which rose to dominance in the central highlands by the 18th and 19th centuries, elevated the Merina dialect of Malagasy as the prestige variety of the island. This consolidation of political power was inseparable from linguistic power — the Merina dialect became the language of governance, literacy, and status, while the languages and dialects of other ethnic groups, including the Sakalava, Betsimisaraka, Tsimihety, and more than a dozen others, were pushed to the margins.

When France colonized Madagascar in 1896, it did not simply impose a foreign language — it layered a second linguistic hierarchy on top of an already unequal one. French became the exclusive language of colonial administration, formal education, and legal proceedings. Speaking Malagasy in official contexts was not merely discouraged; it was structurally eliminated from spheres of power. An entire generation of Malagasy people learned to associate French with opportunity and Malagasy with limitation. This psychological wound — the devaluation of one's own mother tongue — is precisely the mechanism the background guide identifies as the engine of intergenerational transmission failure. When parents fear that speaking their heritage language will invite discrimination or diminish their children's prospects, they stop passing it on.

Independence in 1960 did not resolve this inheritance. Madagascar retained both French and Malagasy as official languages, a practical concession to the entrenched role of French in institutions, commerce, and education. In 1972, the government launched the policy known as Malgachisation, an ambitious attempt to restore Malagasy as the primary medium of instruction in schools. While the intent was to reclaim linguistic sovereignty, the execution was rapid and under-resourced. Teacher training was insufficient, educational materials were lacking, and literacy outcomes suffered during the transition. The policy was largely reversed by the 1990s. The failure of Malgachisation is not evidence that Malagasy cannot function as a language of education — it is evidence that language policy reform without structural investment is destined to fail.

Today, Madagascar maintains an official bilingual policy, but the lived reality is one of severe linguistic inequality. French dominates the formal economy, higher education, and the legal system. Urban youth, particularly in Antananarivo, increasingly communicate in a code-switched blend of French and Malagasy that signals aspirational alignment with globalized professional culture. Meanwhile, the more than eighteen regional dialects of Malagasy — including Sakalava, Betsimisaraka, Antankarana, and Bara — face pressures from two directions simultaneously: from French above, and from the standardization of Merina-based Official Malagasy below. These regional dialects are not merely linguistic variations. They carry distinct oral traditions, proverbs known as *ohabolana*, ceremonial vocabularies, agricultural knowledge systems, and ecological understandings of Madagascar's extraordinary biodiversity — knowledge systems that exist nowhere else on Earth and that cannot be translated without loss. The endangerment of these dialects is a cultural emergency that the international community has not yet fully recognized.

## Past Actions by the Committee

UNESCO has long identified the preservation of linguistic diversity as central to its mission. As early as 1953, UNESCO published *The Use of Vernacular Languages in Education*, a landmark document asserting that children learn most effectively in their mother tongue and advocating for the integration of native languages in primary instruction. This foundational position was reaffirmed and expanded in the 2003 policy paper *Education in a Multilingual World*, which acknowledged multilingualism not as a problem to be managed but as a global reality to be embraced and channeled into educational opportunity.

In 1999, the UNESCO General Conference urged Member States to promote multilingual education as a matter of both pedagogical effectiveness and cultural justice. UNESCO's Multilingual Education (MLE) framework, further developed through International Mother Language Day reports, has demonstrated that children who receive early instruction in their mother tongue demonstrate stronger literacy outcomes, higher academic achievement, and greater long-term engagement with formal education — evidence that the preservation of heritage languages is not in tension with development, but is a condition of it.

Most significantly for the present moment, the United Nations established the International Decade of Indigenous Languages (2022–2032), led by UNESCO, with the explicit mandate to preserve, revitalize, and promote Indigenous languages while embedding linguistic diversity within sustainable development goals. The Decade's Global Action Plan identifies ten interlinked themes, including digital empowerment for indigenous language expression, qualitative education in indigenous languages, and the application of indigenous knowledge to environmental protection. Madagascar, as a member of UNESCO and a signatory to relevant UN frameworks, has participated in these broader initiatives. However, participation in international frameworks has not translated into adequate domestic policy infrastructure, nor has the international community provided the technical and financial support that developing nations require to implement these commitments meaningfully.

The gap between UNESCO's stated commitments and the resources made available to nations like Madagascar to fulfill them is itself a policy failure that this committee must address. Resolutions and decades of action mean little if the burden of implementation falls entirely on nations that lack the institutional capacity to act alone.

## Position of Madagascar

Madagascar comes before this committee not merely as a nation with a linguistic problem, but as a nation whose linguistic situation embodies the full complexity of what this committee exists to address. Madagascar has experienced colonial linguistic erasure, post-colonial identity struggle, internal dialect hierarchies, and the accelerating pressures of digital globalization — all simultaneously. Madagascar's position is therefore both principled and urgent.

Madagascar strongly affirms that mother-tongue-based multilingual education is the most effective and culturally just framework for primary instruction. The evidence is clear, the UNESCO guidance is clear, and the failure of Madagascar's own 1972 Malgachisation effort makes clear what happens when political will exists but structural investment does not. Madagascar calls on UNESCO to move beyond advocacy and into direct technical and financial partnership with developing member states implementing multilingual education reforms.

Madagascar further asserts that the protection of linguistic diversity must extend *within* nations, not only between them. The international community tends to frame language preservation as a matter of protecting national languages from dominant global ones. Madagascar's experience reveals a more complex reality: regional and ethnic dialects within a country can face erasure from a national standard language that has itself been shaped by colonial history. Protecting the Sakalava dialect, the Betsimisaraka dialect, and the other regional varieties of Malagasy is as urgent as protecting Malagasy from the dominance of French. UNESCO's frameworks must explicitly recognize intra-national linguistic diversity as a protected value.

Madagascar also recognizes that the loss of language is never only the loss of words. Madagascar is one of the world's most biodiverse nations, home to species found nowhere else on Earth. The ecological knowledge embedded in regional Malagasy dialects — traditional names, classifications, and management practices for endemic species and landscapes — is irreplaceable. When a dialect dies, that knowledge dies with it. Language preservation and environmental sustainability are not separate agendas; for Madagascar, they are one.

Finally, Madagascar acknowledges the double-edged nature of technology. The expansion of internet access and digital communication holds genuine promise for language documentation and revitalization. At the same time, social media platforms designed in and for dominant languages create structural barriers for speakers of minority languages. Madagascar calls for UNESCO to actively engage the technology sector in developing digital tools — keyboards, speech recognition, translation models, and archival platforms — for endangered and minority languages, with particular attention to languages that have limited written tradition and whose primary form of transmission has historically been oral.

## Possible Solutions

Madagascar proposes the following concrete measures for UNESCO's consideration:

**1. UNESCO Teacher Training Fund for Multilingual Education in Developing Nations**
The failure of Madagascar's 1972 Malgachisation effort was not a failure of vision — it was a failure of capacity. UNESCO should establish a dedicated funding stream for teacher training in mother-tongue-based multilingual education, specifically targeting developing nations with complex multilingual landscapes. This fund should support curriculum development, educator preparation, and material production in heritage and regional languages, with long-term technical assistance rather than one-time grants.

**2. Digital Archiving Initiative for Endangered Dialects**
Drawing on the model of Marie Wilcox's Wukchumni dictionary — which combined audio recordings, written documentation, and community engagement to create a comprehensive language record — UNESCO should fund a global digital archiving initiative prioritizing endangered dialects and oral-tradition languages. For Madagascar, this would mean supporting documentation of regional dialects before speakers are lost. Archives must be community-owned, accessible, and built with the active participation of speakers rather than imposed by outside researchers.

**3. International Guidelines on Language Rights in Education**
UNESCO should develop binding international guidelines establishing the right of children to receive early primary education in their mother tongue, with states required to report on implementation progress. These guidelines should explicitly protect regional and ethnic language varieties within nations, not only nationally recognized official languages. For countries like Madagascar, where a national standard variety has absorbed colonial hierarchies, the protection of regional dialects must be named and enforced.

**4. Community-Led Revitalization Grants**
The Wukchumni case demonstrates that revitalization is most durable when it grows from within a community rather than being administered from above. UNESCO should establish a grants program for community-led language revitalization efforts, modeled on successful cases globally, with low administrative burden to ensure accessibility for grassroots organizations. In Madagascar, this would support local cultural organizations, community radio stations in regional dialects, and youth-facing language programs rooted in cultural identity rather than formal instruction alone.

**5. Technology Partnership for Minority Language Digital Infrastructure**
UNESCO should negotiate with major technology platforms and software developers to require accessibility features for UNESCO-recognized endangered languages, including keyboard support, autocorrect, and content moderation policies that do not penalize non-dominant language use. Additionally, UNESCO should fund the development of AI-assisted language tools — particularly speech recognition and archival transcription — for languages with limited written records, using open-source frameworks that communities can maintain independently.

Madagascar stands ready to work with fellow member states, particularly those in the African Union and the Indian Ocean Commission, to develop regional coalitions in support of these proposals. The diversity of the world's languages is not a relic of the past — it is a living inheritance that belongs to all of humanity. To allow that inheritance to erode through inaction is a failure of the international community's most fundamental obligations. Madagascar urges this committee to act with the urgency this moment demands.
