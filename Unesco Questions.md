## How to Use These Notes

These questions are organized as a research roadmap, not a checklist. Work through each section in order — each layer builds on the one before it. By the time you reach Section IV, you should be able to draw a direct line from Madagascar's pre-colonial linguistic roots → French colonization → post-independence language policy → the internet age.

## Section I — Foundations: Language and Historical Shifts

*Start here. These questions establish the baseline before globalization enters the picture.*

- How does a country's linguistic history reflect the major political shifts it has experienced?
- What is the relationship between language and political power — who decides which language is "official," and why does that matter for ordinary people?
- How does colonization physically interrupt intergenerational transmission? Is it primarily through policy (school mandates, legal restrictions) or through social pressure (discrimination, stigma)?
- When a colonial language is imposed, what happens to the dialects and regional varieties of the indigenous language — do they survive, fragment, or collapse unevenly?
- How do transportation and trade routes shape which languages gain dominance *before* colonization even begins? (Consider: which languages spread because of commerce, and which survived in isolation.)
- How do social customs — ceremonies, oral traditions, naming practices — function as informal language preservation systems?
- What is the difference between a language "dying" and a language being "killed"? Why does this distinction matter for how UNESCO frames revitalization?

## Section II — Madagascar: A Unique Linguistic History

*Madagascar is an unusual case. Dig into why — the answers will make your position paper stand out.*

- The Malagasy language is Austronesian in origin, most closely related to the Ma'anyan language of Borneo. What does this tell us about the pre-colonial migration history of Madagascar, and how did a Southeast Asian language become the dominant tongue of an African island?
- How did the Kingdom of Merina (Imerina) use the Merina dialect of Malagasy as a political tool to consolidate power over other ethnic groups and regions of Madagascar long before French colonization?
- When France colonized Madagascar in 1896, it imposed French as the language of administration, law, and elite education. How did this interact with the *already existing* linguistic hierarchy between Merina Malagasy and other regional dialects?
- Did French colonization reinforce the Merina dialect's dominance (by pushing other dialects further down), or did it displace Merina alongside all other Malagasy varieties? What are the implications of that answer for post-colonial language politics?
- After independence in 1960, Madagascar adopted both Malagasy and French as official languages. What were the political reasons for keeping French? Who benefited from that choice, and who was left out?
- The 1972 "Malgachisation" policy attempted to replace French with Malagasy in schools. What happened to literacy rates and economic opportunity as a result? What does this tell us about the risks of rapid language policy shifts?
- How many dialects of Malagasy exist today, and are any considered endangered relative to Standard Malagasy? What does *intra-language* dialect loss look like?
- How does Madagascar's geographic isolation (island nation, limited early contact with European powers compared to mainland Africa) make its colonial linguistic history different from, say, Tanzania or Senegal?

## Section III — Globalization as a Threat to Linguistic and Cultural Diversity

*These questions go deeper than the background guide's surface treatment. Use them to build your committee speeches.*

### 3A — The Mechanics of Linguistic Erasure Under Globalization

- Globalization is often presented as neutral — the natural flow of people, goods, and information. In what ways is it *not* neutral? Whose languages benefit structurally from globalization, and whose are structurally disadvantaged?
- The background guide describes English as occupying a "hypercentral" position in the global language hierarchy (de Swaan, 2001). What specific mechanisms keep English in that position — trade, military presence, technology platforms, or something else?
- How does economic incentive function as a form of soft linguistic coercion? If a Malagasy speaker knows that French or English opens more job opportunities, is their choice to stop speaking Malagasy truly voluntary?
- How does globalization interact with *existing* colonial-era linguistic hierarchies rather than creating new ones from scratch? Does it accelerate erasure or simply change its texture?
- Can globalization ever work *in favor* of a minority language — for example, by connecting diaspora communities or creating digital archives? What conditions would need to be in place for that to happen?

### 3B — Cultural Diversity as Inseparable from Linguistic Diversity

- The background guide states the connection between language and culture is "undeniable." But what specifically is lost when a language dies — vocabulary, or something deeper? What cultural knowledge is *untranslatable*?
- Oral traditions, proverbs (*ohabolana* in Malagasy), and ceremonial language carry knowledge systems that have no written equivalent. How does the shift to text-based, digital communication threaten oral-dominant cultures disproportionately?
- When a generation grows up consuming global media — social media, streaming, music — in dominant languages, how does this reshape their sense of cultural identity? Is it possible to be culturally Malagasy while primarily communicating in French or English?
- How does the loss of a language erode non-linguistic cultural practices — agricultural knowledge, medicinal knowledge, religious practices — that are embedded in the language itself?
- What is the relationship between biodiversity and linguistic diversity? Madagascar is a global biodiversity hotspot — do indigenous Malagasy languages carry ecological knowledge (about endemic species, land management) that would be lost with the language?

### 3C — The Three-Generation Rule and Youth

- The background guide's "Three-Generation Rule" describes how immigrant heritage languages are typically lost by the third generation. Does this model apply to *non-immigrant* postcolonial settings like Madagascar, where the dominant language was imposed rather than adopted through migration?
- Why are youth particularly vulnerable to language shift under globalization — and are there examples where youth have *led* revitalization rather than being passive agents of language loss?
- How does the attitude of parents and grandparents toward their heritage language shape whether youth speakers emerge? Is the break in transmission primarily a generational or a structural problem?
- If a young Malagasy person sees French as the language of opportunity and education, what argument — economic, cultural, or identity-based — is most likely to make language preservation feel personally relevant to them?

## Section IV — Technology: Double-Edged Sword

*Technology is the most complex angle. It can preserve or destroy — your research should argue which force is stronger, and under what conditions.*

### 4A — How Technology Accelerates Language Loss

- Most of the world's major social media platforms (Facebook, Instagram, X/Twitter, TikTok, YouTube) were designed in English. How does the architecture of these platforms — from autocorrect to content recommendation algorithms — systematically disadvantage speakers of non-dominant languages?
- When a language lacks a standardized digital keyboard, Unicode support, or autocorrect, what practical barriers does this create for young speakers trying to use the language online? How does this affect whether they bother at all?
- Global streaming platforms (Netflix, YouTube) deliver entertainment predominantly in English, French, Spanish, and Mandarin. How does consuming media in a dominant language reshape a young person's linguistic identity over time?
- In Madagascar specifically, how has the spread of internet access (still limited but growing) affected the balance between French, Malagasy, and English among urban versus rural youth?
- Does the spread of smartphones accelerate the shift to dominant-language text communication, particularly in countries where heritage languages have limited written tradition?

### 4B — How Technology Can Protect and Revitalize Language

- Marie Wilcox used a basic computer to create the first comprehensive Wukchumni dictionary. How has technology — from audio recording to database software — transformed what is *possible* for language documentation and preservation?
- What role can digital archives, Wikipedia projects in minority languages, and language-learning apps (such as Duolingo's indigenous language tracks) realistically play in revitalization? What are their limits?
- Is an app-based or online-only revitalization effort sufficient without community-based, in-person transmission? What does the Wukchumni case suggest?
- UNESCO's Global Action Plan includes "digital empowerment and the right of expression in indigenous languages" as one of its ten themes. What concrete policy mechanisms could fulfill this — government-funded keyboard development, mandatory language support in app stores, subsidized media production?
- Could AI language tools (translation models, speech recognition, text generation) be built for endangered languages like minority Malagasy dialects? What would that require, and who should fund it?
- How has radio — a lower-tech but widely accessible medium in rural Madagascar — functioned as a language preservation tool compared to internet-based platforms?

### 4C — Madagascar, Technology, and Linguistic Sovereignty

- Madagascar's internet penetration remains limited, with urban/rural divides shaping who has access to global digital culture. Does this digital divide *protect* rural Malagasy dialect speakers from rapid language shift, or does it simply delay it while creating new inequalities?
- When Madagascar's government or NGOs develop digital tools in Malagasy, they typically use Standard (Merina) Malagasy. What does this mean for the 18+ other regional dialects? Does digital standardization of Malagasy replicate the same hierarchy that French colonization created?
- As Madagascar develops economically and internet access expands, what decisions about language policy and digital infrastructure need to be made *now* — before the window for protecting linguistic diversity closes?
- What lessons can Madagascar draw from the Wukchumni case or from post-Soviet language revitalization efforts in the Baltic states, given its own post-colonial context?
